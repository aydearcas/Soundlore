## RHY | Rhythm, meter and tempo
Summary: This facet covers the organization, perceived movement, and speed of musical time, including beat grouping, rhythmic relationships, and changes in pace.

Rhythm concerns when musical events happen and how their durations and accents relate. Meter provides a recurring framework for grouping beats, while tempo describes their rate. These dimensions interact, but they are distinct: the same rhythm can be performed at different speeds, and a steady tempo can support several patterns of accent. Listeners may perceive a pulse even when no instrument explicitly marks every beat.

The facet has four main branches. *Meter* includes regular beat groupings, changing meters, and overlapping metrical frameworks. *Rhythmic feel* describes how musicians shape the movement of a passage, including straight, swung, half-time, and laid-back approaches. *Rhythmic devices* covers relationships such as syncopation, polyrhythm, and rhythmic displacement. *Tempo* includes broad speed descriptions and ways of changing or stretching the pace.

These features help distinguish musical traditions without defining them alone. Waltzes commonly organize movement around three beats, much rock emphasizes a regular pulse with prominent backbeats, and some West African ensemble traditions combine contrasting rhythmic layers. This facet describes the temporal principles behind such differences. Specific drum grooves and percussion executions belong in *Drums and percussion patterns*; the organization of sections belongs in *Form and structure*. A genre can contain several meters, feels, or tempos.

## DRM | Drums and percussion patterns
Summary: This facet covers recurring drum and percussion grooves, along with playing techniques that shape their accents, textures, and rhythmic impact.

Drums and percussion give rhythm a particular physical and sonic form. A pattern distributes strikes, rests, and accents among instruments or playing surfaces. Its identity depends on more than the placement of beats: differences in touch, sound, timing, and interaction can make similar patterns feel substantially different. Percussion can establish a steady foundation, answer a melody, mark transitions, or become the main musical focus.

The facet is organized into *Drum patterns* and *Drum techniques*. Patterns include recognizable grooves such as four-on-the-floor, dembow, one-drop, and breakbeat-based approaches. Techniques describe how those patterns are performed or embellished, including ghost notes, rolls, flams, fills, brush playing, and linear drumming. A technique can appear within many patterns, while a pattern can be realized with acoustic instruments, electronic drums, or samples.

Percussion is often a strong clue to genre. Reggae one-drop patterns organize emphasis differently from the continuous kick pulse common in much house music. Samba ensembles distribute rhythmic roles across several percussion instruments, creating a collective groove. These examples also show why an isolated pattern cannot identify every recording reliably. General concepts such as meter, syncopation, and tempo belong in *Rhythm, meter and tempo*. Instrument identities belong in *Instrumentation*, while processing and editing percussion sounds belong in *Production and sound*.

## HAR | Harmony and tonality
Summary: This facet covers simultaneous pitch relationships, tonal organization, chord movement, musical closure, and changes between tonal centers.

Harmony concerns how pitches sound together and how those combinations relate over time. Tonality describes an organization in which a particular pitch or chord acts as a center of reference. Music can establish that center strongly, move between centers, combine several, or avoid a clear tonal center. Harmonic tension and resolution depend on musical context and learned expectations; they are not universal measures of emotional meaning.

The facet has six main branches. *Tonality* describes broad systems of pitch organization. *Chords* identifies simultaneous pitch combinations. *Harmonic devices* covers resources such as pedal points, borrowed chords, and reharmonization. *Chord progressions* describes recurring sequences, while *Cadences* concerns gestures of arrival or closure. *Modulation* covers movement between tonal centers. These branches connect individual sonorities with the larger direction of a piece.

Harmony helps distinguish styles through customary resources and their use. Western classical repertories often organize phrases around tonal movement and cadences. Jazz frequently develops extended chords and reharmonization, while blues makes distinctive use of recurring harmonic patterns. These are tendencies rather than requirements for every piece. Pitch collections considered as scales or modes belong in *Scales and modes*. The movement of a melodic line belongs in *Melody and motif*, and the interaction of simultaneous parts belongs in *Texture and counterpoint*.

## SCL | Scales and modes
Summary: This facet covers pitch collections and modal frameworks used to organize melodies, harmonies, and characteristic musical relationships.

Scales provide ways of arranging pitches, often presented in ascending or descending order. Modes describe pitch organization through relationships to a center and characteristic degrees or intervals. A collection of notes does not determine how music will sound by itself: emphasis, melodic movement, tuning, ornamentation, and harmonic context all affect its identity. Two pieces using similar pitch material can therefore have very different characters.

The facet is organized into *Scales*, *Modes*, and *Non-Western and exotic scales*. The first two branches include major and minor resources, pentatonic collections, symmetrical scales, and named modes. The third brings together resources associated with particular musical traditions. Its label is an organizational convenience rather than a claim that these traditions form one musical system. Maqam and raga, for example, involve melodic practices and conventions that extend beyond a simple ascending list of pitches.

Pitch frameworks help describe differences among traditions. Major and minor resources are widespread in Western tonal music; pentatonic collections occur in many musical cultures; Indian classical performance develops melodies within raga frameworks. None of these resources belongs exclusively to one genre. This facet describes the pitch material and its organizing framework. Chord function and tonal movement belong in *Harmony and tonality*, while the actual shaping, ornamentation, and development of melodic lines belong in *Melody and motif*.

## MEL | Melody and motif
Summary: This facet covers recognizable musical lines, recurring melodic ideas, ways of transforming or decorating them, and practices of improvisation.

A melody is a sequence of pitches and durations heard as a connected line. It may be sung, played by an instrument, or distributed among several performers. A motif is a shorter idea that can recur or develop within a piece. Melodic identity depends on rhythm and phrasing as well as pitch, so changing an idea’s timing can alter its character even when its notes remain similar.

The facet has three main branches. *Melodic elements* includes hooks, riffs, motifs, ostinatos, countermelodies, and other recognizable materials. *Melodic devices* describes ways of shaping a line, including sequences, chromatic movement, ornaments, and different relationships between successive notes. *Improvisation* concerns the creation or development of musical material during performance, including practices that respond to an established harmonic or modal framework.

Melodic practices help distinguish traditions and styles. A rock riff can supply a recurring instrumental identity, a jazz solo can develop ideas through improvisation, and Indian classical performance can explore characteristic melodic movements within a raga. The significance of each example lies in how material is used, rather than in the presence of melody alone. Scales and modal frameworks belong in *Scales and modes*. Vocal production belongs in *Voice and vocal technique*, while the placement of melodic ideas across a whole piece belongs in *Form and structure*.

## FRM | Form and structure
Summary: This facet covers the arrangement of musical sections, patterns of repetition and contrast, and established frameworks for complete compositions.

Form describes how a piece unfolds over time. Listeners recognize beginnings, returns, contrasts, transitions, and endings through changes in melody, harmony, rhythm, texture, or sound. A structure may follow a familiar pattern or emerge through gradual development. Understanding form helps explain why a passage feels like preparation, arrival, interruption, or return, even when the listener cannot name the sections involved.

The facet is organized into *Song sections*, *Song forms*, and *Compositional forms*. Song sections identify parts such as verses, choruses, bridges, introductions, breakdowns, and outros. Song forms describe how such parts are arranged and repeated, including verse–chorus and strophic designs. Compositional forms cover broader established frameworks and types of composition, including fugues, rondos, suites, concertos, and other resources used across different repertories.

Formal habits can provide clues to genre. Many popular songs alternate verses and choruses; some electronic dance arrangements organize anticipation around build-ups and drops; Western classical works may use recurring themes within a rondo or a larger sonata framework. Individual works can adapt or combine these practices. This facet concerns the placement and relationship of musical material across time. The material itself belongs in the relevant melodic, harmonic, rhythmic, or textural facet. Recording effects that create a transition belong in *Production and sound*, even when their structural role is described here.

## TEX | Texture and counterpoint
Summary: This facet covers how simultaneous musical parts combine, interact, and distribute melodic or accompanying roles.

Texture describes the overall relationship among musical parts sounding at the same time. Music may present one line, several closely coordinated parts, independent lines, or multiple versions of a shared melody. Texture also concerns density and the distribution of attention: a few widely spaced sounds can feel different from many overlapping parts. The number of instruments alone does not determine the texture.

The facet has three main branches. *Texture* includes broad configurations such as monophony, homophony, polyphony, and heterophony, alongside descriptions of density. *Counterpoint* concerns the interaction of distinct lines, including imitation and voice leading. *Accompaniment textures* describes ways of supporting or responding to a principal part, such as comping, drones, repeated figures, call and response, and antiphonal exchange.

Textural habits can help distinguish musical traditions. Western choral hymns may coordinate voices in chordal movement. Jazz accompaniment can leave space around an improvised lead while contributing its own rhythmic and harmonic activity. Some Indonesian gamelan repertories combine interlocking and layered parts. Such relationships may change within a single piece. This facet describes what the parts do together. Their instrument identities belong in *Instrumentation*, their pitch combinations in *Harmony and tonality*, and their individual melodic ideas in *Melody and motif*. Studio layering belongs here when describing the resulting musical relationship, and in *Production and sound* when describing the recording process.

## VOC | Voice and vocal technique
Summary: This facet covers singing and spoken vocal sounds, voice categories, expressive delivery, and the organization of vocal parts.

The human voice can carry melody, rhythm, language, and expressive character at the same time. Vocal sound depends on how a performer coordinates production, resonance, articulation, and phrasing. Descriptions such as breathy, clean, shouted, or melismatic identify different aspects of delivery rather than fixed categories of singer. A performer may use several approaches within one song, and vocal terminology varies among musical traditions.

The facet is organized into five main branches. *Voice types* describes categories based on range, timbre, or performance role. *Singing techniques* covers approaches such as falsetto, belting, vibrato, and overtone singing. *Harsh vocals* groups deliberately rough or distorted vocal timbres. *Spoken and rapped vocals* concerns speech-centered and rhythmically organized delivery. *Vocal arrangement* describes the relationship among lead voices, backing parts, harmonies, and groups.

Vocal practices are often useful clues to style. Opera commonly emphasizes sustained, projected singing; many R&B performances use melismatic ornamentation; rap foregrounds the rhythmic placement and articulation of words. These practices also travel across genre boundaries. This facet describes vocal delivery and the organization of voices, rather than the literary content of the words, which belongs in *Lyrics and prosody*. Electronic pitch correction, vocoding, and recorded vocal distortion belong in *Production and sound* when the focus is the processing method. Melodic shapes shared with instrumental music belong in *Melody and motif*.

## INS | Instrumentation
Summary: This facet covers the instruments, sound-producing resources, and ensembles used to perform or construct music.

Instrumentation describes which sound-producing resources take part in music. These may include acoustic instruments, electronic devices, percussion, or established ensembles. Instruments contribute characteristic ranges, attacks, resonances, and possibilities of expression, but their identities do not prescribe a single musical role. A guitar can provide melody, chords, bass movement, or percussion-like gestures, and electronic instruments can imitate familiar sounds or produce unfamiliar ones.

The facet is organized into *Plucked strings*, *Bowed strings*, *Woodwinds*, *Brass*, *Keyboards*, *Electronic instruments*, *Percussion*, and *Ensembles*. These branches provide practical routes through instrument families and performance groupings. They are browsing categories rather than one universal scientific classification: keyboard instruments, for example, produce sound through several different mechanisms. Electronic resources also include recognizable devices and sounds associated with particular production practices.

Instrument combinations can strongly suggest a tradition. A string quartet identifies a familiar Western chamber ensemble; mariachi often combines strings with trumpets; Indonesian gamelan ensembles prominently use tuned percussion. Similar instruments may nevertheless appear in very different genres, and substitutions can preserve a musical role while changing its sound. This facet identifies the resources involved. How performers play them belongs in *Instrumental techniques*, the relationship among their parts in *Texture and counterpoint*, and recording or processing choices in *Production and sound*. Vocal delivery is developed separately in *Voice and vocal technique*.

## TEC | Instrumental techniques
Summary: This facet covers ways of playing and manipulating instruments that shape articulation, pitch, rhythm, timbre, and musical expression.

Instrumental technique describes the actions through which a performer produces and shapes sound. Different gestures can change a note’s attack, duration, pitch, resonance, or rhythmic emphasis. Techniques may be associated with a particular instrument, but related musical effects can arise through different physical methods. A connected phrase, for example, requires different actions on a guitar, a bowed string instrument, and a wind instrument.

The facet is organized into *Guitar techniques*, *Bass techniques*, *Keyboard techniques*, *Bowed-string techniques*, *Wind techniques*, and *DJ techniques*. These branches include picking and muting approaches, bass articulation, keyboard accompaniment patterns, bowing and plucking gestures, breath-related effects, and the manipulation of recorded material through turntables. Some entries describe recurring performance approaches, while others identify a particular gesture or sound-producing method.

Techniques help give genres their recognizable detail. Flamenco guitar makes distinctive use of strumming and plucking approaches such as rasgueado and picado. Jazz bass often supplies motion through walking lines. Hip-hop turntablism uses scratching and related manipulation as musical performance. These resources can be adopted outside their customary settings. This facet focuses on performance methods. Instrument identities belong in *Instrumentation*, broadly applicable articulations belong in *Dynamics and articulation*, and audio processing such as compression, filtering, or reverberation belongs in *Production and sound*. A processed sound may combine contributions from both playing technique and studio treatment.

## PRD | Production and sound
Summary: This facet covers recording choices, audio processing, sampling, sound design, and production aesthetics that shape the audible character of music.

Production shapes how performed or constructed sounds become a recording or amplified presentation. Decisions about capture, editing, processing, balance, and space can change the listener’s experience substantially. Production may preserve the impression of an immediate performance or create a sound that could not arise from an unprocessed ensemble. Its contribution is audible even when listeners cannot identify the equipment or methods involved.

The facet organizes these choices through several branches. *Recording aesthetic* and *Era aesthetic* describe broad sonic approaches and historical references. *Dynamics processing* concerns signal-level control, while *Spatial effects* creates or modifies perceived space. *Modulation effects*, *Distortion*, *Filters*, and *Pitch effects* alter different properties of sound. *Time manipulation* changes timing, duration, or playback direction. *Sampling* reuses recorded material, and *Sound design* develops sounds for particular musical roles.

Production practices can help distinguish styles. Dub frequently gives echo and reverberation an active musical role. Some punk recordings emphasize a direct, relatively rough presentation. Contemporary pop often uses detailed editing and layered vocal production. These are tendencies, and recordings within each tradition can differ widely.

This facet concerns how sound is captured, transformed, and presented. The performer’s physical actions belong in *Instrumental techniques* or *Voice and vocal technique*. Musical loudness and articulation as expressive performance choices belong in *Dynamics and articulation*. Historical sound labels describe selected production traits rather than everything recorded during a particular decade.

## DYN | Dynamics and articulation
Summary: This facet covers expressive changes in musical intensity and the ways notes are attacked, sustained, connected, or separated.

Dynamics concerns the relative strength or intensity of musical events. Articulation concerns how notes begin, continue, and end, including their connection or separation. Together, they shape phrasing and emphasis: the same sequence of pitches can sound restrained, forceful, flowing, or sharply marked depending on its execution. Their effects depend on musical context, instrument, and the contrasts established within a passage.

The facet has two main branches. *Dynamics* includes quiet and loud performance, gradual increases and decreases, sudden emphasis, and contrasts between passages. *Articulation* includes approaches such as staccato, legato, marcato, and tenuto. These terms describe relationships within the music rather than fixed acoustic measurements. A quiet passage can remain intense, while a strong attack need not produce a long or sustained sound.

Expressive handling helps distinguish performances and styles. Western classical repertories often use carefully shaped dynamic contrasts and articulation. Jazz phrasing can depend on subtle accents and differences in note length. Rock arrangements may create dramatic contrast between restrained verses and forceful choruses. No single pattern defines all examples of a genre. This facet describes musical expression through intensity and note shaping. Compression and limiting belong in *Production and sound* as signal-processing methods. Instrument-specific ways of achieving an articulation belong in *Instrumental techniques*, and emotional interpretations of the result belong in *Mood and character*.

## LYR | Lyrics and prosody
Summary: This facet covers the verbal construction of songs, including rhyme, sound patterns, narrative devices, and the relationship between words and musical delivery.

Lyrics contribute language, imagery, narrative, and verbal sound to music. Prosody concerns how features such as syllables, stress, rhythm, and phrasing interact with musical delivery. Words can align closely with musical accents or create expressive tension against them. Their meaning also changes through repetition, placement, and performance, so a lyric’s musical effect cannot be understood from its written text alone.

The facet is organized around *Lyrical devices*. This branch includes rhyme schemes, internal and multisyllabic rhyme, assonance, repetition, storytelling, wordplay, vocables, and other ways of shaping verbal material. It also includes broad approaches to lyrical content, such as protest, confession, and abstraction. These entries describe resources that can coexist: a narrative song can use repeated phrases, intricate rhyme, and ambiguous imagery at the same time.

Lyrical practices help distinguish traditions and individual styles. Rap often gives particular prominence to rhyme placement and verbal rhythm. Folk ballads frequently organize words around a developing story. Some flamenco singing stretches or repeats words for expressive emphasis. These practices are shared and adapted across genres. This facet concerns the words and their organization in music. Vocal timbre and delivery belong in *Voice and vocal technique*, while melodic ornamentation belongs in *Melody and motif*. An instrumental track has no sung or spoken lyric content, even when its title suggests a narrative.

## MOO | Mood and character
Summary: This facet covers the perceived emotional and expressive qualities of music, including atmosphere, energy, intimacy, tension, and dramatic character.

Mood and character describe how music is experienced rather than a single measurable feature of its construction. Listeners may hear a passage as playful, ominous, contemplative, triumphant, or intimate because of interacting musical cues and personal associations. Rhythm, timbre, register, harmony, dynamics, and words can all contribute. Interpretations also depend on cultural familiarity, listening circumstances, and what has happened earlier in the piece.

The facet is organized under *Mood*. Its concepts provide a vocabulary for broad expressive impressions, including uplifting, melancholic, aggressive, dreamy, mysterious, and spiritual qualities. Several descriptions can apply simultaneously or to different moments of one recording. A piece may combine energetic movement with dark timbres, or a restrained performance with strong emotional intensity. These terms therefore support description without imposing a single emotional identity.

Character can help compare genres, but its value depends on context. Listeners may hear playful energy in funk, solemnity in a devotional chant, or grandeur in an orchestral passage. None of these impressions belongs exclusively to that tradition. This facet records the perceived expressive result. The rhythmic, harmonic, instrumental, and production features contributing to it belong in their respective facets. Bright timbre and a bright mood, for example, are related descriptions with different meanings. Mood labels should not be treated as automatic consequences of major or minor tonality, tempo, or instrumentation.
